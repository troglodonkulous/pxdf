Floor App Cutover

How to build the macOS app around the verified Python sender, in the days before the 10 × 12 job — what to keep untouched, what to cut, and the one render pass that every geometry feature falls out of.

repo ~/dev/ddf @ 2612822 revised 2026-08-11 job rig 8x8_4P 10 × 12

The decision

Don't port the mapper. It is the only thing in this project graded against hardware — 80/80 frames exact against Ledset's 68/72. A Swift or JS rewrite restarts the exact risk CLAUDE.md exists to prevent, and buys nothing: the job rig is 7,680 pixels at 30 fps.

Ship Electron + TypeScript + React, with the existing Python as a sidecar. The mockups are working HTML on a real token system, the compositor is native Canvas2D, and the whole integration is one new ~30-line Python subcommand.

Composite at 8× floor resolution and downsample once, at the end. This is the architectural centre of the app — it is where the resolution finding, rotation, mirror, master brightness and auto-fit all live, in a single pass.

Electron is not a dead end. Syphon and NDI arrive later as a sidecar-protocol helper — 2 to 4 days — not as a rewrite. Nothing verified moves.

Controller topology is identical. Ten tile columns at four ports per controller derives three controllers with a partial last one, at both geometries — so the shop rig genuinely rehearses the job rig's controller layer, EOF placement included.

What differs is depth: five packets per port instead of two, last one 281 bytes. Packet index tops out at 127, so there is no ceiling problem. And what the shop rig does not rehearse is the physical arrangement — twelve tiles deep per chain against five. Controller ordering is decided by cabling at load-in and nothing in this repo can predict it. Budget a white frame and a per-column walk before content goes near the floor.

The render pipeline

The 1280 × 640 finding, generalised — and the pass that every geometry feature belongs to.

Generating content at 80 × 40 evaluates the image once per floor pixel. Every edge aliases, and sub-pixel motion does not exist: a shape moving a sixteenth of a pixel per frame produces no change at all until it crosses a boundary. Generating at 1280 × 640 and area-downscaling averages 256 samples per floor pixel, which buys two things that are easy to conflate:

  • Clarity — antialiased edges instead of hard-quantised ones.
  • Smoothness — sub-pixel motion becomes visible, because a shape moving a sixteenth of a pixel changes the average even when it crosses nothing. This is why smoothness improved with no change in frame rate.

2560 × 1280 was not better because 1,024 samples per pixel is indistinguishable from 256 — the ordinary supersampling curve. This is the same physics as --scaler area beating neighbor in RUNSHEET §11 P4‑5, now measured a second time from the opposite direction.

Rule

Floor resolution is an output format, never a project format. Composite at floor_px × S, downsample once at the very end with an explicit box filter, and store nothing at 80 × 96.

Downsampling per-layer, or handing a 1920 × 1080 video straight to an 80 × 96 canvas and trusting drawImage, re-lives the neighbor problem inside the browser — at 24:1 Chromium's filter is not a true area filter.

One pass owns every geometry feature

Rotation, mirror, master brightness and the downsample are all the same loop: read the work canvas, write the wire framebuffer, parameterised by (rotation, mirrorX, mirrorY, S, master). Write it once, test it once, and Rotation, cue rotation, cue mirror and the resolution fix all fall out of it together.

Python owning the clock is not stylistic. It is the double-buffering requirement from the README, for free: a renderer hitch re-sends the previous frame instead of producing a gap, and the wire keeps its measured ±0.4 ms pacing regardless of what Chromium is doing.

Picking S

The 16× sweet spot was measured through make_clip.py --scaler area — different code from the app's downsample, and judged partly on file size and generation time, neither of which exists in the app. The principle transfers; the number has to be re-measured, or it becomes exactly the borrowed-instrument mistake this project keeps making.

So S is a setting — 4 / 8 / 16, default 8 — shown next to a live render-milliseconds readout, and settled at the shop against real clips. Cost scales as S²: at S = 8 the job rig's work canvas is 640 × 768 and the downsample is ~492k reads per frame, comfortable in plain JavaScript. At S = 16 it is ~2M reads and wants a WebGL pass.

Generators inherit all of this free. The loop console's renderFrame is already a pure function of normalised (bx, by, phase01), so it renders at whatever the work canvas happens to be — the validated math is unchanged.

Store normalised, display in pixels

The Xform panel's "values in floor pixels — small enough to think in pixels" is a good display choice. The stored value must be normalised 0–1, or every geometry change corrupts every cue. Store normalised, render × floor_px, and re-fit becomes a re-render rather than a migration.

That is what makes auto-fit affordable, and it matters here more than usual: 10 × 5 is 2:1 landscape and 10 × 12 is 5:6 portrait. Auto-fitting cover between those crops roughly 60% of the frame. Flagging rather than silently rescaling is right, and per-cue cover / contain / stretch is the minimum.

Loose ends worth closing first

One changes what you build; the rest are cheap.

Custom layouts break the address arithmetic — and Ledset can't express them either

shelved

The mapper derives a pixel's wire address as tile_col = x // tile_px, then controller, port = divmod(tile_col, ports_per_controller). Both assume a full rectangle: every tile column carries exactly tile_rows tiles, every port payload the same length.

A ragged layout means ports with differing payload lengths, and nothing in this project has ever put that on the wire. The vendor's mapper XML is three integers — a type index and two tile-count indices — so Ledset cannot describe an L-shaped floor at all. No reference implementation, no capture that constrains the answer.

Ship the rectangle path. Keep the Layout control with Custom disabled and a "needs a floor session" note.

mapper/frame.py:36–48 · mapper/mapper_xml.py:26–34 · reference/Test3.mapper.xml

"Rotation" and "orientation" are different layers — keep the names apart

naming · CLAUDE.md

mapper/orientation.py holds rotate180, mirrorX and reverseColumnsPerController. Those are cabling compensation, applied at the wire-mapping stage to correct for how the floor was physically patched. CLAUDE.md settles that layer as identity, permanently, and it stays settled.

Setup's Rotation is a different thing entirely: a presentation transform, so an operator can author a physically 80 × 96 portrait floor as a 96 × 80 landscape canvas. It lives in the final composite pass, and the mapper still receives an 80 × 96 row-major RGB framebuffer at every rotation.

Zero changes to mapper/. The wire mapping stays identity, which is precisely what CLAUDE.md protects. Add a note there distinguishing the two, or a future session will merge them and produce the seventh self-consistent-but-wrong result.

CLAUDE.md "Settled — do not reopen" · mapper/orientation.py

Blackout must send black frames, not stop sending

design requirement

Controller 1 is the Master/IR unit and reverts to its own local content roughly 5 s after packets stop; controllers 2 and 3 hold their last frame indefinitely. Stopping the send during a blackout does not black the floor — it produces a floor showing three different things, one of them vendor demo content, at a wedding.

The socket opens with the show file and never closes. BLACKOUT, cue gaps, load screens and idle are all black frames at 30 fps — the gapless-loop rule extended to every state the app can be in.

Brightness and Master have no controller-side implementation

compositor-side

Protocol Q6 is closed: Ledset applies no transform on the video-to-wire path, and a full string sweep of the vendor stack found no colour or brightness control reachable over Ethernet for this controller. Both faders are pixel multiplies — and they belong in the final pass, alongside the downsample, so the Python path stays byte-transparent and the digest check below keeps its meaning.

Import normalisation — smaller problem than first flagged

corrected

Every clip in Test Clips, Test Clips 2 and ddf-loop-res-test probes as H.264 yuv420p, including the .mov files — MP4-family containers, not ProRes. Chromium plays all of it natively. The codec warning applies only to the uncompressed AVIs in test-patterns/.

Transcode-on-import drops from blocker to normaliser, and it should transcode at source resolution, not floor resolution — the Xform panel needs real pixels to scale from, and the supersampled work canvas needs them even more. Rates in the library run 25 / 30 / 50 fps, so MAX_ADOPTED_FPS = 30 will cap the 50s, which is the settled behaviour.

The integration seam

One new subcommand. Nothing existing is edited.

run() already takes a Callable[[int], bytes], and pack_send_buffer already expects a row-major RGB framebuffer of pixel_count × 3 bytes — which is exactly what the final pass produces.

# mapper/cli.py — new subcommand, ~30 lines
def cmd_stream(args) -> int:
    config = _config(args)
    nbytes = config.pixel_count * 3
    latest = bytearray(nbytes)          # opens black
    lock = threading.Lock()

    def reader():
        while (buf := sys.stdin.buffer.read(nbytes)) and len(buf) == nbytes:
            with lock:
                latest[:] = buf

    threading.Thread(target=reader, daemon=True).start()

    def frame_source(n: int) -> bytes:
        with lock:
            return bytes(latest)   # stall → resend, never a gap

    run(config, frame_source, duration=None, fps=args.fps,
        orientation=Orientation.parse(args.orientation),
        address=args.address, port=args.port, local_ip=args.local_ip)
    return 0

Add --emit-digest to print frame 0's sha256 to stderr, so the gate below runs without a packet capture.

The gates you already own

Render gradient24 in the app's own compositor with putImageData — exact values, no filtering — at the shop geometry, rotation 0, no mirror, and push it through the pipeline. Frame 0's digest must be:

Same digest that already grades the play/send cutover. It runs off-hardware in seconds, and it catches the failure mode this project is prone to: a compositor that produces a plausible picture through a wrong mapping. The final pass gets three more that cost nothing:

  • rotate 90° four times → identity
  • mirror on either axis twice → identity
  • rotation 0, mirror none, S = 1, master 100% → the digest above, unchanged

Which means every geometry feature you add is graded against the same digest that graded the Ledset cutover.

Electron is not a dead end

The sidecar architecture already makes the renderer the swappable part. The app is three separable pieces, and only one of them is Electron:

PieceLives inLifespan
Wire protocolPython, hardware-verifiedpermanent
Show modelplain JSONpermanent
Renderer + UIElectronreplaceable

Syphon and NDI are frame sources, and the sidecar already accepts frame sources. A ~200-line Swift helper that receives a Syphon texture, downsamples it and writes RGB to the same stdin byte contract makes Electron the control surface and the helper the video path. For Syphon as a compositable layer rather than a full-floor takeover, the helper writes into shared memory and the renderer draws it like any other layer — one extra hop, a few milliseconds, at 7,680 pixels.

PathCostWhat moves
Syphon / NDI helper2–4 daysNothing verified. New process, existing byte contract.
Native macOS rewrite3–5 weeksUI and compositor only — protocol and show model transfer intact.

Two rules cost nothing today and are what keep the second row from ever becoming mandatory: the show file is plain JSON with no DOM or Electron types in it, and the renderer-to-sidecar frame protocol is a documented byte contract, not an implementation detail.

If you later want native for feel rather than capability, that decision gets made with a working product and real usage behind it — the best possible time to make it, and a UI rewrite rather than a product rewrite.

The cue editor splits in two

The compositor — don't build it, and you won't have to. Canvas2D's globalCompositeOperation covers Normal / Add / Multiply / Screen exactly as the inspector lists them; ctx.filter covers hue, saturation, contrast and brightness, globalAlpha covers opacity and one composite pass covers tint — that is the whole of Requirements §4, per layer, for almost nothing; drawImage with a transform covers the Xform panel. The eleven generators are a plain loop over a typed array, and the work canvas at S = 8 is 492k pixels — WebGL only becomes necessary at S = 16.

The text generator is the same story: fillText and strokeText at work resolution, plus a scroll offset. One design consequence is worth building around rather than discovering — at 80 px wide the floor fits about ten 8-px glyphs, so text here is a scrolling marquee, not a static title. The four scroll directions in Requirements §5 are the feature, not a garnish on it.

The timeline widget — build it, it's smaller than the libraries. Four lanes, one clip each, drag-to-trim, reorder, playhead, collapse. That is After Effects' layer panel, not an NLE — roughly 400 lines of React over absolutely-positioned divs. Every candidate is heavier and none matches the DDF tokens:

  • xzdarcy/react-timeline-editor — MIT, 779 stars, last pushed 2026-01-25. Closest fit, but brings a visual language you would spend a day overriding.
  • remotion — 56k stars, actively developed, but a programmatic render framework, and its licence requires a paid company licence for for-profit organisations above three employees.
  • openvideodev/react-video-editor, twick — full editors with export pipelines you don't want, both under non-standard licences.

Cut from this build

Named so they don't creep back in on day three.

Not in ship 1

  • Custom / L-shaped layouts — shelved. Unproven on the wire, inexpressible in the vendor format. Needs a floor session before it is even a feature. Struck through in Requirements §1 independently.
  • Reverse and Bounce playback — shelved. Requirements §2 and the Play mockup both carry it, but Chromium refuses a negative playbackRate and a frame cache is ~1.7 GB for a 30 s clip at work resolution. The cheap path when it comes back is an ffmpeg -vf reverse pass at import, so direction becomes a different file rather than a different decoder; WebCodecs is the upgrade beyond that. Ships forward-only, with the Direction control visible and disabled.
  • Masking — shelved. Requirements §3 lists shape and layer masks; nothing in the mockups designs a control for it. Called out so it is a decision rather than an oversight.
  • Effects processing — the FX tab ships present and empty. Requirements §7 wants per-source and per-layer effects over a parameter set, plus an architecture that accepts more later. The tab is in the mockups so it ships as a tab; what sits behind it is deferred, because the same PR already carries text, generators, Setup, auto-fit and crash recovery. The colour half of §7's parameters lands anyway as §4 — see below.
  • Sound-reactive — shelved. Audio capture plus a mapping to brightness, intensity and speed is a feature of its own. Space is held in the Play page's cue panel, per Requirements §12.
  • Colour calibration — scrapped, not shelved. Built, merged and reverted with cause on 2026-08-11; the defect is a load-dependent 5 V rail droop that no per-region constant can track. A Calibrate… button in a mockup is not permission to reopen it.
  • Syphon / NDI — deferred, not blocked. Arrives as a sidecar-protocol helper, 2–4 days, whenever it is wanted.
  • WebGL generators — conditional. Only needed if S = 16 wins at the shop. Plain JS covers S = 8.

When this recommendation is wrong

  • If a booked event needs a non-rectangular floor. Custom layouts stop being shelvable, and the first work is a hardware session probing whether a controller renders a short port payload — not app code.
  • If S = 16 turns out to be necessary and JS can't hold 30 fps. Then the final pass moves to a WebGL shader. The pass is designed so that swap touches one file, but it is a day that isn't in the plan.
  • If the 10 × 12 rig doesn't cable the way the shop rig does. Controller ordering is set at load-in and nothing here predicts it. The white-frame and per-column walk are not optional.
  • If frame pacing turns out to matter more than measured. The 7.8%-of-a-frame worst-case deviation at 60 fps is documented and unexplained. Harmless at 30; an open question if the app ever needs 60.
  • If the app must run unattended for hours. Nothing here is soak-tested. Day four's drills are the minimum, not proof.
